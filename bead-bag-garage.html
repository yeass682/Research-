Shop rule #1

Trust the torque spec, not the guy at the gas station. The engine lineups and published outputs here are checked against manufacturer and historical sources. But nobody publishes a complete, engine-by-engine count of the U.S. pickups still on the road: not us, not the DMV, not that one forum guy. The percentages in the bag are modeled assumptions, not registration data. We show the math at the bottom. Move the sliders and argue with it, because that's what it's there for.

    Shop notes: how the bag gets filled

    This is a thought experiment that shows its work. It doesn't come from a proprietary fleet database. The idea is that a model you can poke at is more honest than a confident-looking percentage with nothing behind it.

    weight(engine) = Σmodel years [ assumed sales index × assumed take rate × 2−age ÷ half-life ]
    chance(engine) = weight(engine) ÷ weight(every engine in the bag)

    The rust factor is a heuristic you control. It's not a measured survival curve, and the 22-year default is a modeling choice, not a real truck survival estimate. A 2024 research paper put the median U.S. pickup lifetime at roughly 25 years, and the DOE cited a 13.1-year average pickup age in 2023. Neither one sets a universal half-life. Annual volumes and option mixes also change from year to year, and manufacturers rarely publish them broken out by engine.

    Verified
    Factory engine families, rough availability eras, and current manufacturer-rated outputs where they're stated.
    Assumed
    Historical take rates, annual-volume indices, and which old trucks are still out there.
    Not in the bag
    Engine swaps; heavy-duty variants (except the labeled Titan XD); foreign-market oddities; separate EV-only pickups.
