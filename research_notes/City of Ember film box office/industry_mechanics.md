## 1. Roles: producer, financier, studio, distributor, exhibitor; what "wide release" means; how theaters are booked

### Takeaway
The distributor licenses each film to theaters one theater and one film at a time. In 2008 most US deals used a sliding-scale formula fixed before opening, firm terms, or an after-the-run "settlement." In contested areas the distributor chooses one theater per "film zone." Exhibitors (Regal, AMC, Cinemark) keep the rest of the ticket money and almost all concession revenue. Advertising for a major release is organized and paid for by the distributor.

### Cited Findings
- **Booking process (Regal, the largest US chain, FY2008 10-K, filed early 2009):** "In zones where there is competition, a distributor will allocate films among the exhibitors in the zone. When films are licensed under the allocation process, a distributor will select an exhibitor for each film who then negotiates film rental terms directly with the distributor." Where Regal is the only exhibitor in a zone, it "obtain[s] film licenses by selecting a film from among those films being offered and negotiating directly with the distributor." — [Regal Entertainment Group Form 10-K, fiscal year ended Jan 1, 2009](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
- **Film zones (Cinemark FY2008 10-K):** Films are licensed "on a theatre-by-theatre and film-by-film basis." "For new release films, film distributors typically establish geographic zones and offer each available film to one theatre in each zone. The size of a film zone is generally determined by the population density, demographics and box office revenues potential." "New film releases are licensed at the discretion of the film distributors." Cinemark operated in 242 first-run film zones and was the sole exhibitor in about 86% of them. — [Cinemark Holdings Form 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
- **How long a film stays (Regal FY2008):** "The duration of our film licenses are negotiated with our distributors on a case-by-case basis... Marketable movies that are expected to have high box office admission revenues will generally have longer license terms than movies with more uncertain performance." — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
- **Who pays for advertising (Regal FY2008):** "Currently, film distributors organize and finance multimedia advertising campaigns for major film releases." — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
- **Why theatrical matters to the whole chain (Regal FY2008):** "The theatrical success of a film is often the most important factor in establishing its value in other film distribution channels... the value of home video, DVD and pay cable distribution agreements frequently depends on the success" of the theatrical run. Cinemark's 10-K makes the same point: a successful theatrical release "brands" a film for "downstream" markets such as DVD, TV, VOD and pay-per-view. — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm); [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
- **Size of the big chains in 2008:**
  - Regal: 552 theatres, 6,801 screens, 245.2 million attendance, average ticket $7.68 in fiscal 2008 (a 53-week year). — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
  - Cinemark: 3,742 US screens in 293 theatres (third-largest US circuit), plus 1,041 screens in 12 Latin American countries; 211.3 million patrons in 2008. — [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
- **Industry totals for 2008:** about 1.36 billion US/Canada tickets at an average price of $7.18, the NATO-sourced figure the MPAA later reprinted. — [The-Numbers 2008 market summary](https://oak1.the-numbers.com/market/2008/summary); [MPAA 2012 Theatrical Market Statistics (historical table)](https://www.motionpictures.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/2012-Theatrical-Market-Statistics-Report.pdf)
- **Studio = financier + distributor (Epstein, 2010):** Epstein said in an interview that the major studios are in two businesses, making films and distributing them, and that for the large studios these are effectively the same. — [NPR, "Crunching Numbers in the Hollywood Economy," Apr 1, 2010](https://www.npr.org/2010/04/01/124535538/crunching-numbers-in-the-hollywood-economy) (from the search summary; I did not read the full transcript)

### Inferences
- In plain terms for City of Ember:
  - The **producer(s)** developed and made the film. Walden Media was the producing/financing partner, and Playtone is commonly credited. Confirm credits with the film-specific researcher.
  - The **financier(s)** paid the roughly $55M production budget.
  - The **studio/distributor** (20th Century Fox, through the Fox-Walden label) booked it into theaters, shipped prints, bought the advertising, and collected film rentals.
  - **Exhibitors** sold the tickets and popcorn and sent the distributor its share.
- "Wide release" has no single legal definition. In common trade usage around 2008 it meant a nationwide opening on roughly 600+ screens, and big studio releases often opened on 2,000–4,000. I found no authoritative 2008 definition in this session, so treat the threshold as convention (see Gaps). An opening of about 2,000+ theaters is a mid-sized wide release. Blockbusters of the era opened on 3,500–4,000+.

## 2. Box office splits ("film rentals"): how much of the gross reached the distributor in 2008

### Takeaway
2008 SEC filings from the two biggest public chains show US exhibitors paying distributors about **52–56% of admissions**. That figure includes the exhibitors' own (small) advertising costs, so pure film rent was slightly lower. The share is front-loaded: the distributor takes a higher percentage in a film's early weeks and the percentage declines over the run. Rentals are **lower abroad**. The usual rule of thumb is about 40% internationally, with wide variation by country.

### Cited Findings
- **Regal fiscal 2008:**
  - Film rental and advertising costs were $990.4M on admissions of $1,883.1M, which is **52.6% of admissions** (53.1% in 2007; 52.5% in 2006).
  - Regal explains the 2008 decline: "primarily the result of a lower percentage of box office revenues generated by the top tier films exhibited... and a decline in advertising expense." This shows that blockbusters carry higher rental rates.
  - Source: [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
- **Regal's description of the deal formulas (FY2008 10-K):** "The primary formulas used are the 'sliding scale' formula, a 'firm term' formula and a 'review or settlement.' Under a sliding scale formula, the distributor receives a percentage of the box office receipts using a pre-determined and mutually agreed upon film rental template. This formula... is the predominant formula used by us... Under the firm term formula, the exhibitor and distributor agree prior to the exhibition of the film on a specified percentage... under the review or settlement method, the exhibitor and distributor negotiate a percentage... upon completion of the theatrical engagement. These negotiations typically involve the use of historical settlements or past precedent." — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
- **Cinemark 2008:**
  - US film rentals and advertising were $494.6M, **55.6% of admissions** (55.2% in 2007).
  - Consolidated US + Latin America: $612.2M, 54.3% of admissions.
  - Cinemark's firm terms "reflect either a mutually agreed upon aggregate rate for the life of the film or rates that decline over the term of the run." Its sliding scale is "a pre-determined matrix based upon box office performance of the film."
  - Source: [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
- **International rentals (Cinemark, Latin America, 2008):** "Internationally, our film rental licenses are primarily based on mutually agreed upon firm terms established prior to the opening of the picture. The film rental percentages paid by our international locations are generally lower than in the U.S. markets and gradually decline over a period of several weeks." — [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
  - (my calc, from search-result figures I did not re-verify in the full segment table) Cinemark's international film rentals and advertising of about $117.6M against about $237M of international admissions works out to roughly 49–50%. Treat this as unverified. It is also the exhibitor's payment to the *local distributor*, which may itself be a third party taking a fee before the studio's net.
- **International rule of thumb [LATER PRACTICE / WEAK SOURCES]:**
  - A trade-oriented summary states the studio keeps "50% in the US, 40% internationally, and 25% in China."
  - A survey of foreign territories put typical settlement rates at 36–46% for the major territories (China about 25%).
  - Source: [Blockbuster movie economics 101 (Substack)](https://brandstofans.substack.com/p/blockbuster-movie-economics-101-and?open=false); see also [Celluloid Junkie, Nov 4, 2017](https://celluloidjunkie.com/2017/11/04/disney-can-demand-65-box-office-star-wars-last-jedi/) [LATER PRACTICE]. These are post-2010 descriptions. China's 25% cap is mostly relevant post-2012 and not to a 2008 family film.
- **Epstein (Slate, May 16, 2005), worked example:**
  - On *Gone in 60 Seconds*' $242M worldwide gross, theaters kept $129.8M and Buena Vista got $101.6M after MPAA dues.
  - Theater owners' retained share "can amount to more than 50 percent of the original box-office total."
  - That means a studio's worldwide average share can be **below 50%** once foreign markets are included.
  - Source: [Edward Jay Epstein, "Gross Misunderstanding," Slate, May 16, 2005](http://www.slate.com/articles/arts/the_hollywood_economist/2005/05/gross_misunderstanding.html)

### Inferences
- **Domestic rentals:** A reasonable 2008 US assumption is that the distributor received **about 50–55% of domestic gross on average**. The share was higher for a film that earned most of its money in its first two weeks, and lower for one that played a long time. Regal/Cinemark's 52.6–55.6% (rent + exhibitor advertising) brackets this.
- **Front-loaded films:** A film like City of Ember that opened modestly and dropped fast would earn most of its gross in the high-percentage early weeks. Its effective rental rate would therefore probably not be far below average. This is a hedged inference; the actual terms are confidential.
- **International rentals:** A reasonable 2008 assumption is **about 40% of international gross (range about 35–50%)**. Where a local third-party distributor was used, the studio/financier net could be lower still.
- **"90/10" deals:** The "90/10" form (distributor gets 90% of receipts above a negotiated house "nut") is often described for that era, but **I did not find a 2005–2010 primary source in this session that confirms it**. The 2008 10-Ks describe "sliding scale," "firm terms" and "settlement" instead. The report should describe the mechanism by the 10-K terms and not assert 90/10 without a source.

- I found no published, film-specific rental terms for City of Ember. These are not public.
- I found no authoritative 2008 figure for average international rental by country.

## 3. Prints & advertising (P&A): marketing spend and 35mm print costs in 2007–2008

### Takeaway
The MPAA's last published average was **$35.9M of marketing per member-studio film in 2007**. Average production (negative) cost was $70.8M, for a combined $106.6M. The MPAA stopped publishing these averages after that. In 2008 nearly all US screens still ran 35mm film. Industry estimates put a print at **roughly $1,000–$1,500**, which means about $2–3M in prints for a 2,000-theater opening.

### Cited Findings
- **MPAA 2007 averages:**
  - The average advertising (marketing) cost for an MPAA-member studio film rose 4% to **$35.9M in 2007**.
  - The average production cost rose 8% to **$70.8M**, for a combined average of **$106.6M**.
  - Source: [Variety, 2008, "MPAA: Specialty films see rising costs" (from search snippet; paywalled when fetched)](https://variety.com/2008/film/markets-festivals/mpaa-specialty-films-see-rising-costs-1117981882); [Studio Daily, March 2008, "Movie budgets on the up and up"](https://studiodaily.com/2008/03/movie-budgets-on-the-up-and-up)
- **MPAA stopped reporting:**
  - ArtsJournal/LA Times reported that the MPAA "won't disclose the average amount of money that studios spent on making and marketing movies last year," calling it a reversal of longstanding practice. This was about the 2008 numbers, in the report released in early 2009.
  - A March 12, 2010 blog post notes that "for the second year in a row" the MPAA omitted average production and marketing costs.
  - Together these indicate that **2007 was the last year published**.
  - Sources: [ArtsJournal, "Suddenly Secretive: MPAA Won't Reveal Spending Numbers"](https://www.artsjournal.org/?p=44812); [Chris Thilk, "Still no marketing numbers," Mar 12, 2010](https://christhilk.com/2010/03/12/still-no-marketing-numbers/)
- **Epstein (Slate, May 16, 2005):**
  - "The cost of prints and advertising for the opening of a studio film in America in 2003 totaled, on average, $39 million." He says that was $18.4M more per film than studios recovered from US box-office receipts.
  - Studios spend "$20 million to $40 million on TV ads" for an opening.
  - *Gone in 60 Seconds* needed "$13 million for prints so the film could open in thousands of theatres."
  - Source: [Epstein, Slate, May 16, 2005](http://www.slate.com/articles/arts/the_hollywood_economist/2005/05/gross_misunderstanding.html)
- **Epstein (NPR, 2005):** Studios "on average... spend more on advertising than they get back in box office receipts." — [NPR/WYSO, June 7, 2005](https://www.wyso.org/2005-06-07/is-hollywood-wasting-money-selling-movies-to-teens)
- **35mm print cost and staggered releases (Smith & Telang, Carnegie Mellon, May 2016, describing the analog era):**
  - "In the era of analog projection, films were distributed to theaters on 35mm reels, delayed international releases were driven primarily by an effort to economize on the cost of producing and distributing film prints (around $1,500 per reel)."
  - Used prints from the US were refurbished "at a cost of a few hundred dollars" and sent abroad.
  - Note: the authors say "per reel," but the context is a full print.
  - Source: [Smith & Telang, "Windows of Opportunity," CMU, May 2016](https://www.cmu.edu/entertainment-analytics/documents/effectiveness-of-anti-piracy-efforts/windows-of-opportunity.pdf)
- **Other print-cost estimates:**
  - Epstein, in a 2010 NPR interview about *The Hollywood Economist*, estimated prints at "a thousand dollars a print" (from search summary; not read in full). — [NPR, Apr 1, 2010](https://www.npr.org/2010/04/01/124535538/crunching-numbers-in-the-hollywood-economy)
  - A search summary of an academic paper gave $750–$1,200 per print, but I could not tie that figure to a specific paper URL, so treat it as unverified.
  - A Film-Tech exhibitor forum poster estimated about $2,000. [WEAK SOURCE] — [Film-Tech forum](https://www.film-tech.com/ubb/f16/t000518.html)
- **Digital conversion had barely started in 2008:**
  - Regal, AMC and Cinemark formed Digital Cinema Implementation Partners (DCIP) on Feb 12, 2007, "to create a financing model and establish agreements with major motion picture studios for the implementation of digital cinema." As of early 2009 DCIP was still arranging financing. — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
  - Cinemark: "The industry has begun to convert to the use of digital projection technology." — [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
  - Under the later virtual print fee (VPF) model, studios paid exhibitors per digital booking to share the savings from not striking film prints. [LATER PRACTICE] — [Wikipedia, Virtual Print Fee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_Print_Fee)

### Inferences
- **Prints:** For a release on about 2,000+ US screens in Oct 2008, prints probably cost **about $2–3.5M** (about 2,000–2,300 prints × $1,000–$1,500) (my calc). That excludes shipping, trailers and other materials.
- **US marketing for City of Ember:** The MPAA's $35.9M 2007 average is skewed upward by blockbusters. A plausible bracket for a mid-budget 2008 family release is **about $20–40M domestic P&A**. This is an inference, not a sourced figure for this film. International P&A would be extra and is often handled per territory.
- **Total spend:** All-in, a $55M film with, say, $30M+ of domestic P&A plus international P&A represents roughly $90–110M+ of total spend before distribution fees and overhead (my calc, illustrative).

- MPAA averages for 2008 do not exist publicly, because the MPAA stopped publishing them.

## 4. Release windows in 2008 and the size and decline of DVD

### Takeaway
In 2008 a film normally reached DVD about **4 to 4½ months** after its theatrical opening. City of Ember's DVD came out Jan 2009, about 3 months later; confirm that date with the film-specific researcher. DVD was the studios' biggest profit source in the mid-2000s. US home-entertainment spending peaked at **$24.1B in 2006** and fell to **about $22.4B in 2008** as DVD sales slid with the recession. Blu-ray grew but was too small to offset the decline.

### Cited Findings
- **Window length:**
  - NATO data: "In 1997, the average video release window was five months and 22 days"; "In 2009, the time from the movie theater to DVD was four months and 11 days."
  - Fox's 2009 average was three months and 29 days, and Sony's four months and four days.
  - Source: [Knowledge@Wharton, "Reel Time: The Incredible Shrinking Window," Nov 24, 2009](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/reel-time-the-incredible-shrinking-window-for-movie-releases/)
  - A Hollywood Reporter piece (2009) cited a NATO-tracked average of 129 days for 2009 releases (search snippet). — [Hollywood Reporter, 2009](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/paramount-dvd-release-dates-angers-90073)
  - A Yale/Cowles paper: the window shortened "from about 7 months in 1998 to about 4.5 months in 2005" (search snippet). — [Cowles Foundation paper](https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-08/d2325_0.pdf)
- **Exhibitors on the window (Regal FY2008):** "Over the past several years, the average period between a film's theatrical release and its in-home video or DVD release has remained relatively stable." Regal warned that "a material contraction of the theatrical release window could significantly dilute the consumer appeal." — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm)
- **DVD's dominance (Epstein, 2005):**
  - "DVDs alone now provide 59 percent of the feature film revenues of the studios, as opposed to 48 percent in 2004."
  - Studios earned "$5.67 billion dollars from DVD sales" in Q1 2005 versus $4.375B in Q1 2004.
  - Moviegoers provided studios "less than 15 percent of their worldwide revenues."
  - Source: [Epstein, "Hollywood's Death Spiral," Slate, July 25, 2005](https://slate.com/culture/2005/07/hollywood-s-death-spiral.html)
  - In a separate piece: "Ticket sales from theaters provided 100 percent of the studios' revenues in 1948; in 2003, they accounted for less than 20 percent... home entertainment provided 82 percent of the 2003 revenues" (home entertainment here includes video, DVD and TV). — [Epstein, Slate, May 16, 2005](http://www.slate.com/articles/arts/the_hollywood_economist/2005/05/gross_misunderstanding.html)
  - Caution: these percentages come from the MPAA's confidential "All Media Revenue Report" via Epstein and are **gross revenues to studios**, not profits.
- **DVD decline in 2008 (Digital Entertainment Group (DEG) figures, released Jan 2009 at CES):**
  - "DVD rentals and sales peaked at $24.1 billion in 2006 and have slid to $22.4 billion in 2008." — [Knowledge@Wharton, Nov 24, 2009](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/reel-time-the-incredible-shrinking-window-for-movie-releases/)
  - Secondary reports of the DEG data: total spending fell about 5.5% to $22.4B. Sell-through fell about 9% to about $14.5B, rentals were flat at about $7.5B, and Blu-ray spending rose about 178% to about $750M.
  - Sources disagree slightly (5.5% vs 5.7%; 9% vs 9.5%), likely because they measured different categories. — [TheWrap, Jan 2009](https://www.thewrap.com/dvd-sales-way-down-high-def-slow-rescue-1404); [Computerworld, Jan 2009](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1476826/blu-ray-disc-content-market-almost-tripled-in-us-in-2008.html)
- **Later windows:** Regal lists the downstream channels after theatrical as "home video and DVD, cable television, broadcast television and satellite and pay-per-view services." The Wharton article describes pay-per-view, subscription channels such as HBO, and cable as the later windows but gives no durations. — [Regal 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1168696/000104746909002095/a2191042z10-k.htm); [Wharton, Nov 2009](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/reel-time-the-incredible-shrinking-window-for-movie-releases/)

### Inferences
- **Why DVD matters for City of Ember:** For a 2008 film, the theatrical gross was in effect the advertisement for DVD and TV. A theatrical loss could be partly or fully recouped through DVD and TV, especially for family titles, which historically sold well on DVD. City of Ember's DVD arrived in Jan 2009, at the start of the post-2006 decline and in a recession year. Each DVD sold returns far more to the studio per unit than a ticket does. The film-specific researcher should check DVD unit sales (e.g., The-Numbers' video charts).
- **Typical later windows (common practice, but not sourced in this session):** pay-TV (e.g., HBO/Starz) about 6–12 months after theatrical, free TV after about 2–3 years. Treat these as unverified.

- I found no sourced 2008 durations for the pay-TV and free-TV windows.

## 5. Break-even rules of thumb

### Takeaway
The often-repeated heuristic is that a film needs a worldwide box-office gross of about **2–2.5× its production budget** to break even on its theatrical run. The logic: about half the gross goes to theaters, and P&A is often comparable to a large fraction of the budget. Every careful source frames it as a rough heuristic, and it ignores DVD/TV income, which in 2008 often turned theatrical losses into profits.

### Cited Findings
- "The often-repeated rule that a movie needs to gross around 2 to 2.5 times its production budget can be useful as a quick estimate, but it is not a universal formula." The source adds that a film's true break-even point "can be affected by marketing spend, domestic versus international gross, and tax incentives." — [GeekVibesNation explainer](https://geekvibesnation.com/how-much-does-a-movie-need-to-make-to-be-profitable/) [WEAK SOURCE; undated, likely post-2015]
- **Epstein's 2003 averages show why theatrical alone loses money:** average US P&A of $39M exceeded the studio's US box-office receipts by $18.4M per film. — [Epstein, Slate, May 16, 2005](http://www.slate.com/articles/arts/the_hollywood_economist/2005/05/gross_misunderstanding.html)
- Stephen Follows and Bruce Nash define "breakout" low-budget films as those that "make far more profit than is typical for films of their scale." This is a profit framing, not a fixed multiple. [LATER PRACTICE, retrospective] — [Stephen Follows/AFM](https://americanfilmmarket.com/producer-resource/what-types-of-3m-10m-films-break-out/)

### Inferences
- **Illustrative theatrical-only check for a $55M film (my calc, all assumptions labeled):**
  - Assumptions: domestic rentals at 50–55% of domestic gross; international rentals at about 40% of international gross; domestic P&A $25–40M; international P&A $10–20M (unsourced assumption).
  - Distribution fees (commonly cited at about 30% domestic/35–40% foreign in that era, but **not sourced this session**) and studio overhead are ignored.
  - On those terms, break-even needs rentals of roughly $90–115M.
  - With a 50/50 domestic/foreign split, that implies a worldwide gross of about **$190–250M, or about 3.5–4.5× budget**. The bare 2–2.5× rule therefore looks generous once P&A is counted.
  - Conversely, any worldwide gross far below about $110M (2× budget) almost certainly means a theatrical loss.
- The report should present the 2–2.5× heuristic as a **lower bound of plausibility** with the above caveats. It should also note that DVD and TV revenue could recover part of a theatrical loss.

### Gaps
- I found no high-quality, 2005–2010 dated source stating the 2–2.5× rule, such as a Variety/Hollywood Reporter explainer or Vogel. Attribute it as a common industry heuristic.
- I found no sourced 2008 distribution-fee percentages.
- I did not retrieve any Stephen Follows piece stating a break-even multiple.

## 6. International rollout logic

### Takeaway
In the 35mm era, studios typically opened in the US first and rolled the film out abroad over weeks or months. The main reasons were to save on expensive prints (US prints were reused abroad), to wait for local holidays and school calendars, to allow dubbing, subtitling and censorship, and because foreign exhibitors wanted to see US performance first. The cost of the delay was more piracy. Studios distributed through their own international arms in major territories and through local licensees elsewhere.

### Cited Findings
- **Smith & Telang (CMU, May 2016), on the analog era:**
  - "Studios have historically also staggered their release windows across geographic markets, typically releasing movies first in the United States and later in Europe, Asia, South America and other smaller markets."
  - "Studios would print a limited number of reels for their highest value market (typically the domestic U.S.), and then after the movie left theaters in the U.S., would clean and refurbish these reels at a cost of a few hundred dollars and send them to theaters in international markets."
  - "International exhibitors also frequently ask for delayed releases so they can judge the quality of movies based on their performance in the other countries. Local holidays, marketing constraints, editing/censorship, and dubbing/subtitling also play a role in international delays."
  - Source: [Smith & Telang, CMU, May 2016](https://www.cmu.edu/entertainment-analytics/documents/effectiveness-of-anti-piracy-efforts/windows-of-opportunity.pdf)
- **Piracy cost of staggering:** Danaher & Waldfogel estimated that pre-release piracy, enabled by US-to-foreign lags, cut international box office by at least 7% (via search summary of the paper). — [Danaher & Waldfogel working paper (hosted copy)](https://media-alliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/SSRN-id19862991.pdf)
- **International was already the majority of worldwide gross:** "International markets... accounting for $17.1 billion, or 64% of 2007 total worldwide box office revenues according to MPAA." — [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)
- **Local terms:** International exhibitor rentals were "primarily based on mutually agreed upon firm terms established prior to the opening" and were "generally lower than in the U.S. markets and gradually decline over a period of several weeks" (Cinemark, Latin America). — [Cinemark 10-K FY2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385280/000095013409005288/d66662e10vk.htm)

### Inferences
- **Staggered dates:** For City of Ember, a staggered foreign rollout (dates spread from late 2008 into 2009) would be normal for the era. It would especially fit a film whose US opening was soft: foreign distributors might shrink or delay releases.
- **Different deal structures abroad:** Walden Media's co-financed films were sometimes sold territory-by-territory to independent distributors abroad rather than released through Fox International everywhere. If so, the financier's international income would come as minimum guarantees and overages rather than studio rentals. This needs verification by the film-specific researcher.
- **What "global" meant then:** In 2008 (35mm, pre-DCIP), "day-and-date" global releases were reserved for tentpoles. Most mid-budget films rolled out.

## 7. How weekend box office reports work; per-theater average; "legs"; second-weekend drops

### Takeaway
Studios report weekend estimates on Sunday. Those combine actual Friday and Saturday ticket sales with a projected Sunday, and are revised to actuals on Monday. In 2008 the theater-level tallies were collected by Nielsen EDI, sold to Rentrak in 2009–2010 (Rentrak was later absorbed by comScore). Box Office Mojo, The-Numbers and the trades publish the figures. Per-theater average (weekend gross ÷ theaters) and week-to-week percentage drops measure "legs."

### Cited Findings
- "Studio estimates for the weekend are released Sunday morning between 9 and 10 a.m. Pacific Time... Sunday data is projected using Saturday's confirmed numbers." Monday actuals follow after Sunday sales are tallied. One cited example estimate of $100.2M became $98.7M in actuals. — [FindThisMovie, "How box office numbers are actually reported"](https://findthismovie.com/how-box-office-numbers-are-actually-reported/) [WEAK SOURCE; current-era, undated]
- Today's figures are "collected directly from theater point-of-sale systems and reported by Comscore (formerly Rentrak)." [LATER PRACTICE] — same source
- Epstein (2005) criticized reported box-office figures as studio-supplied numbers that matter little to studio profits. — [NPR/WUSF, "Slate's Hollywood Economics: Bogus Box Office Numbers," May 16, 2005](https://www.wusf.org/2005-05-16/slates-hollywood-economics-bogus-box-office-numbers)

### Inferences
- **Per-theater average:** For a layperson, opening weekend gross ÷ theater count gives the per-theater average. Around 2008, a wide release averaging well under about $3,000–$5,000 per theater on opening weekend was generally read as weak. This is a convention, not sourced in this session.
- **Legs:** A film's "multiplier" (total domestic gross ÷ opening weekend) measures legs. Family films often hold better than teen or genre films, but no 2008 statistic was retrieved.
- **Second-weekend drops:** Typical second-weekend drops for family films are commonly characterized as about 35–50%. I found **no sourced 2008 average** for this; the report writer should rely on the film's actual Box Office Mojo/The-Numbers week-by-week data rather than a generic benchmark.
